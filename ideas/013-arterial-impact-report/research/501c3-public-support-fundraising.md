# IRS Public-Support Requirements & Fundraising Strategy for a 501(c)(3) Arts/Media Nonprofit

*Research prepared for Arterial — a 501(c)(3) media organization supporting the arts, currently substantially founder-funded.*

> **Disclaimer:** This is research, not legal or tax advice. The public-support rules are mechanical in places and genuinely fact-and-circumstances in others. Any §-level decision — which test to elect, how to treat a specific grant, whether a gift qualifies as an "unusual grant," how close the org is to "tipping" — should be confirmed with a nonprofit CPA or tax attorney who can model Arterial's actual five-year Schedule A.

## Executive Summary — the direct answer to the client's question

**"It can't be that the founder just puts in $100K of his own money to keep the nonprofit's status. Does showing 500 people donating $1 each satisfy the IRS more than 2–3 large individual donations?"**

The client's instinct is exactly right, and the IRS math is unambiguous:

- **The founder's $100K does the *opposite* of protecting public-charity status.** A 501(c)(3) that is funded mainly by one person looks — to the IRS — like a *private foundation*, not a public charity. The founder is a "disqualified person" / "substantial contributor," and his gifts are the single biggest drag on the public-support ratio (see below).
- **500 people giving $1 each beats 2–3 large individual gifts — decisively.** Under the most common public-support test (IRC §170(b)(1)(A)(vi)), any single private donor's gift counts toward "public support" **only up to 2% of the organization's total support over a rolling 5-year period.** Everything above that 2% cap still sits in the denominator (total support) but is *thrown out of the numerator* (public support).
- **The concrete math (worked in Section 2):** On $1,000,000 of 5-year total support, the 2% cap is $20,000 per donor.
  - **500 small donors** each well under the cap → counted **in full** → strong public-support ratio (50% in the worked example).
  - **3 large donors** giving the same total → only $20,000 of *each* counts → public-support ratio collapses to **6%** — a failing grade.
  - Same dollars raised. Wildly different IRS outcome. **Breadth of the donor base, not total dollars, is what the test rewards.**

**Why it works this way:** The public-support test exists precisely to distinguish charities that draw *broad public support* from those that are effectively one person's or one family's private vehicle. The 2% cap is the mechanism. It is deliberately designed so that "500 people at $1" is worth more than "2 people at $250,000."

**Two important nuances that soften this for an arts org:**
1. **Government grants and grants from other public charities are NOT subject to the 2% cap** — they count *in full* as public support. A city arts-agency grant or a grant from a large publicly-supported arts institution is a full-value public-support dollar.
2. **Earned/program revenue (tickets, admissions, class fees, media licensing) can count as public support — but only under the *other* test, §509(a)(2)**, not under §170(b)(1)(A)(vi). For a media/arts org that can generate mission-related earned income, §509(a)(2) is often the better-fitting classification.

**Bottom line for Arterial's fundraising goals:** the goal is not "raise $X." The goal is **"raise $X from as broad a base as possible, while shrinking the founder's share of total support below ~10–15% over the rolling five-year window."** An annual impact report is a direct instrument of that goal — it recruits and retains the small and mid-level donors whose gifts are worth the most to the test.

## 1. The Public Support Test — Mechanics

Every 501(c)(3) is **presumed to be a private foundation** unless it qualifies as a public charity under one of the categories in IRC §509(a). Two of those categories are relevant here, and each has its own "public support test."

### Path A — IRC §170(b)(1)(A)(vi) / §509(a)(1): the "donative" charity

This is the classic path for organizations funded mainly by **gifts, grants, and contributions** from the public and from government.

- **The 33⅓% test (mechanical):** If **public support ÷ total support ≥ 33⅓%** over the measurement period, the org automatically qualifies.
- **The 10% facts-and-circumstances test (fallback):** If the ratio falls below 33⅓% but is **at least 10%**, the org may still qualify by demonstrating — qualitatively — that it operates like a public charity (diverse sources of support; continuous, bona-fide solicitation of the general public; a broadly representative governing body; programs that serve the public). Below 10%, this fallback is unavailable and the org is a private foundation. *This is the genuinely fact-and-circumstances part of the regime — there is no bright line.* (Treas. Reg. §1.170A-9(f).)

### Path B — IRC §509(a)(2): the "program-service" charity

This path fits organizations funded substantially by **exempt-function revenue** — admissions, ticket sales, tuition, workshop/class fees, performance or media-licensing fees. It has **two** simultaneous fractions:

1. **Public support > 33⅓%** of total support, where public support = contributions + membership fees + **gross receipts from mission-related activities** (subject to a per-payer cap — see §2), **and**
2. **Investment income ≤ 33⅓%** of total support (gross investment income + net UBTI may not exceed one-third).

Both conditions must be met every year.

### Which test, which fraction

| | §170(b)(1)(A)(vi) / §509(a)(1) | §509(a)(2) |
|---|---|---|
| Public-support threshold | ≥ 33⅓% (or 10% + facts/circumstances) | > 33⅓% |
| Earned/program revenue in numerator? | **No** — only in denominator | **Yes** — counts as public support |
| Per-donor contribution cap | 2% of total support | 2% of total support (contributions); **greater of $5,000 or 1%** per payer on gross receipts |
| Second test (investment income ≤ 33⅓%)? | No | **Yes** |
| Schedule A part | **Part II** | **Part III** |

### The measurement period — the rolling 5-year window

Both tests are computed over a **5-year period: the current tax year plus the four immediately preceding tax years.** It is recomputed every year on a rolling basis, so any single large gift affects the ratio for five consecutive filings. (IRS: "both tests measure public support over a five-year period.")

### Schedule A of Form 990 / 990-EZ

**Schedule A ("Public Charity Status and Public Support")** is the attachment where the org proves its status each year.

- **Part I** — checkbox for the reason for public-charity status (which §509(a) category); this determines whether you complete Part II or Part III.
- **Part II** — support schedule for **§170(b)(1)(A)(vi)** orgs: five year-columns + total, rows for gifts/grants/contributions, then computed **public support %** = public support ÷ total support.
- **Part III** — support schedule for **§509(a)(2)** orgs: same five-year structure, but computes **both** the public-support % (must exceed 33⅓%) and the investment-income % (must not exceed 33⅓%).

Both Form 990 and Form 990-EZ filers use the same Schedule A.

## 2. The 2% Limitation — why many small donors beats a few big ones

This is the heart of the client's question.

### The rule

Under §170(b)(1)(A)(vi), when you build the **public-support numerator**, a contribution from any one private donor (individual, corporation, private foundation, or non-publicly-supported charity) counts **only up to 2% of the organization's total support for the 5-year computation period.** Anything above 2% is "excess" — it remains in **total support (denominator)** but is excluded from **public support (numerator)**.

**Not subject to the 2% cap (counted in full):**
- **Government grants** (grants from governmental units — as distinct from fee-for-service contracts).
- **Grants from other public charities** that themselves qualify under §170(b)(1)(A)(vi)/§509(a)(1).

*(Exception: if a donor "earmarks" a gift through a government unit or public charity to benefit your org specifically, the 2% cap can still apply because the donor is really directing the support.)*

### Worked illustration — $1,000,000 of 5-year total support ⇒ 2% cap = $20,000 per donor

**Scenario A — 500 small donors.** 500 donors × $1,000 = $500,000; remaining $500,000 from non-public sources (e.g., investment income). Each $1,000 gift is 0.1% of total support — far below the 2% cap — so **all $500,000 counts.**
> Public support ratio = $500,000 ÷ $1,000,000 = **50%** → **passes** (≥ 33⅓%).

**Scenario B — 3 large donors, same total.** Donor A $400,000, Donor B $300,000, Donor C $300,000 = $1,000,000. Each is capped at $20,000 in the numerator.
> Public support = $20,000 × 3 = $60,000. Ratio = $60,000 ÷ $1,000,000 = **6%** → **fails** (below even the 10% floor).

**Same $1,000,000 raised. 50% vs. 6%.** The only difference is *breadth*. This is the precise, literal answer to "does 500 people at $1 satisfy the IRS more than 2–3 large donations?" — **yes, overwhelmingly.**

**Scenario C — why a big *government* grant is different.** Government grant $400,000 + one individual $600,000, total $1,000,000. The government grant counts **in full**; the individual is capped at $20,000.
> Public support = $400,000 + $20,000 = $420,000. Ratio = **42%** → **passes.** A single large *government* dollar is a full public-support dollar; a single large *private* dollar is 2%.

### The founder, disqualified persons, and substantial contributors

This is where founder self-funding actively hurts.

- A **"substantial contributor"** is anyone whose cumulative gifts exceed 2% of the org's total support since inception. The founder who puts in $100K almost certainly *is* one.
- The founder, board members, officers, their families, and entities they control are **"disqualified persons"** (IRC §4946). They are **not treated as "the public."**
- Their contributions **do not help the numerator** (they are capped at 2% like any private donor, and much of the founder's large gift is excess) **but they fully inflate the denominator** — mathematically the worst of both worlds. A large founder gift simultaneously (a) adds little to public support and (b) enlarges total support, *pushing the ratio down.*
- **Related-donor aggregation:** gifts from related family members and controlled entities are aggregated and treated as one donor for the 2% cap — you cannot split a big founder gift across relatives to dodge the limit.

**Takeaway:** the founder writing a $100,000 check is, from a public-support standpoint, close to counter-productive — it can *lower* the very ratio it's meant to protect. What protects status is a *wide base of modest external gifts.*

## 3. Timeframe & Consequences

### The first-five-years grace period (post-2008 rules)

Before 2008, new charities got a temporary "advance ruling" and later had to prove public support. That process was **eliminated in 2008.** Under current rules, a new 501(c)(3) that represents on its exemption application that it *expects* to meet a public-support test is **treated as a public charity for its first five tax years** — with no obligation to actually pass the test or compute it on Schedule A during that window.

- Starting in **year 6**, it must satisfy the applicable test every year, computed on the rolling 5-year window (which by then includes years from the grace period).
- Reclassification, if it ever happens, is **not retroactive** to the grace-period years.

### The two-consecutive-year cushion (the "cure" period)

The rules include a built-in buffer so one bad year doesn't cost you your status:

- If the org **passes** the test for a given year, it is treated as publicly supported for **that year and the following year**, regardless of the following year's actual numbers.
- **Effect:** you generally must **fail two consecutive years** before losing public-charity status.
- Illustration: pass Year 6 → covered for 6 and 7. Fail Year 7 → still a public charity (Year 6 carries it). Fail Year 8 → no prior passing year to lean on → reclassified as a private foundation beginning Year 8.

### "Tipping"

**Tipping** is when a charity with modest, broad-based support receives gifts so large (often from one donor, family, or a single grant) that they dominate the 5-year computation and drive the public-support ratio below the threshold — "tipping" the org into private-foundation status. A heavy founder gift, or one outsized foundation grant, is a classic tipping risk.

### Consequences of reclassification to private foundation

The org stays a 501(c)(3), but falls under the **Chapter 42 excise-tax regime (IRC §§4940–4945)** and loses the flexibility of public-charity status:

- **§4940 — excise tax on net investment income** (annual tax on investment income).
- **§4942 — minimum distribution / ~5% payout:** must distribute roughly 5% of non-charitable-use asset value annually or face a **30% excise tax** on undistributed income (additional tax if uncorrected; excess distributions carry forward up to 5 years).
- **§4941 — self-dealing:** strict prohibitions on transactions between the foundation and disqualified persons; excise taxes on the disqualified person and, sometimes, managers.
- **§4943 — excess business holdings; §4944 — jeopardizing investments; §4945 — taxable expenditures** (e.g., improper grants, lobbying).
- **Reduced donor deductibility:** cash gifts deductible up to **30% of AGI** (vs. 60% for public charities); appreciated capital-gain property up to **20% of AGI** (vs. 30%), often at basis rather than fair market value. This makes the org *less* attractive to the very major donors it might be leaning on.

## 4. Practical Fundraising Implications for a Small Arts Nonprofit

### Do grants count as public support?

- **Government grants → count in FULL** (no 2% cap) under both tests. The single most efficient public-support dollar available. Prioritize city/county/state arts agencies, NEA, NEH, public-media funds.
- **Grants from other public charities** that qualify under §170(b)(1)(A)(vi)/§509(a)(1) → **count in full** (no 2% cap). Large publicly-supported arts foundations and institutions.
- **Private-foundation grants → capped at 2%** of total support per grantor (in the numerator) under §170(b)(1)(A)(vi). Valuable, but don't let any one private foundation dominate. A very large, one-off, unexpected private grant from a disinterested source *may* qualify as an **"unusual grant"** and be excluded from *both* numerator and denominator — which prevents it from wrecking the ratio (but this does not apply to founder gifts, since the founder is not disinterested).

### Broad small-dollar fundraising — the workhorse

Because every donor under the 2% cap counts **in full**, small-dollar breadth is the most efficient way to build public support:

- **Crowdfunding** (season/production/exhibit campaigns), **low-tier memberships** ($5–$25/month), **small recurring gifts** (Patreon-style tiers), **First-Friday micro-donations.**
- Each does triple duty: (1) adds fully-counted numerator dollars, (2) shrinks the founder's / any major donor's *share* of total support, (3) builds the qualitative record for the 10% facts-and-circumstances fallback (continuous, bona-fide public solicitation).
- Membership dues structured as contributions (minimal tangible benefit) count as public support.

### Does earned revenue help? — only under §509(a)(2)

- Under **§170(b)(1)(A)(vi):** ticket sales, admissions, tuition, licensing = **excluded from the numerator**, included in the denominator. Growing earned income *hurts* this ratio.
- Under **§509(a)(2):** the same mission-related receipts **count as public support** (subject to the greater-of-$5,000-or-1%-per-payer cap). For a media/arts org building screenings, performances, classes, and licensing, **§509(a)(2) is frequently the better classification** — the revenue you're already building becomes public support. This is a decision to confirm with a nonprofit CPA and, if a change is warranted, handle correctly on Schedule A / with the IRS.

### Major gifts & founder reliance

- Major external gifts are still worth pursuing (mission impact, matching, capacity), but assume most of each large gift is **excess** above 2% and won't lift the ratio.
- **Founder funding:** treat as seed capital / gap-filling backstop, not a pillar. Target the founder's share **below ~10–15% of total support** across the rolling 5-year window. Every external small gift raised lets the founder's percentage fall without cutting a smaller check.

### A workable target mix for a small arts/media 501(c)(3)

Sector data show earned income is often the largest single revenue line for nonprofit arts orgs, with contributions and grants making up the rest; diversification is the consistent recommendation. A practical target:

| Source | Target share | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Mission-related **earned revenue** (tickets, classes, licensing) | 30–50% | Public support under §509(a)(2); builds sustainability |
| **Broad individual giving** (small gifts, memberships, recurring) | 20–40% | Fully-counted numerator; breadth for facts-and-circumstances |
| **Institutional grants** (gov't, public charities, private foundations) | 20–30% | Gov't & public-charity grants uncapped; diversify funders |
| **Founder / insider** support | < 10–15% | Doesn't help numerator; keep from dominating denominator |

Plus: **review Schedule A every year** to track both the 5-year public-support % and the founder's/large-donor share, and get a nonprofit CPA to confirm §170(b)(1)(A)(vi) vs. §509(a)(2) is the right classification.

## 5. How an Impact Report Supports This

An annual impact report is not just PR — for a founder-funded arts org it is a **donor-diversification instrument**, and donor diversification is exactly what the public-support test rewards.

### Why it moves donors (and thereby breadth)

- **Outcomes, not activity.** Donors respond to *change* (access created, youth served, voices platformed), not output counts alone. Pair "42 screenings" with "reached 3,800 first-time attendees across 11 neighborhoods."
- **Dollars → results linkage.** "$100 funded X workshop-hours" makes small gifts feel consequential — which recruits and retains exactly the sub-2% donors the test values most.
- **Donor-centric framing + gratitude.** Credit supporters for outcomes; feature small and recurring donors, not only major ones.
- **Financial transparency.** A clean revenue/expense summary builds the trust that converts one-time givers into recurring members.
- **Close with momentum.** A forward roadmap tied to specific giving opportunities (recurring tiers, sponsorships, project gifts) drives the next wave of breadth.

### The metrics that *also* document public-support breadth

Report these — they double as fundraising proof and as internal public-support evidence:

- **Total number of donors**, and the **share of support from small/mid gifts vs. major donors.**
- **Donor tiers** (e.g., <$100 / $100–$999 / $1,000+) — visually demonstrates that many donors give under the 2% line.
- **Geographic spread** of donors and audiences (maps/heatmaps) — evidence of broad, non-concentrated community backing (useful for facts-and-circumstances).
- **Community-reach metrics** (participants, audiences, students, online viewers) and **diversity of funding sources** (individuals / foundations / government / earned).
- **Stories and testimonials** with consent — the emotional layer that makes the metrics land.

The same donor-tier and source breakdown the development team presents in the report is what finance uses to monitor the year-by-year Schedule A ratios. **One artifact, two jobs:** it grows the base *and* documents the breadth that keeps the org publicly supported. Repurpose it all year — thank-yous, campaign updates, grant proposals, board decks.

## Implications for Arterial's Fundraising Goals

1. **Reframe the goal.** Not "raise enough to cover the budget," but "**raise from as many external donors as possible while driving the founder's share of 5-year total support below ~10–15%.**" Breadth is the compliance metric, not total dollars.

2. **The founder's self-funding is a status *risk*, not a safeguard.** A $100K founder check adds almost nothing to public support and inflates the denominator — it can lower the ratio it's meant to protect. Structure future founder support as loans, in-kind, or a backstop, and confirm the treatment with a CPA.

3. **Decide the test deliberately.** As a *media* org that can generate mission-related earned income (screenings, licensing, classes, events), **§509(a)(2) may fit better than §170(b)(1)(A)(vi)** because earned revenue becomes public support. This is a CPA conversation before the grace period ends.

4. **Chase the uncapped dollars first.** Government grants and grants from large public charities count in full — the highest-leverage public-support dollars available. Build a diversified institutional-grant portfolio; don't let any one private foundation dominate.

5. **Make small-dollar breadth a standing program.** Memberships, recurring micro-gifts, First-Friday donations, annual crowdfunding — each fully-counted, each shrinking the founder's share.

6. **Watch the clock.** If Arterial is in its first five years, it has a grace window — but the year-6 test uses a 5-year window that reaches *back into* the grace years. Start building breadth now so year-6 numbers are already safe. Remember the two-consecutive-year cushion, but don't rely on it.

7. **Instrument the impact report for the test.** Publish donor counts, tiers, geographic spread, and source diversification annually. It recruits the sub-2% donors that matter most and simultaneously documents the breadth Schedule A needs.

### Where the rule is genuinely fact-and-circumstances (flag for the CPA)
- The **10% facts-and-circumstances test** has no bright line — qualification is a judgment call on the totality of solicitation, source diversity, and governance.
- **"Unusual grant"** treatment (excluding a large one-off gift from the ratio) depends on the donor being disinterested and the gift being unexpected/non-recurring — never applies to founder gifts.
- **§170(b)(1)(A)(vi) vs. §509(a)(2)** classification, and whether/how to change it, is a professional determination with Schedule A consequences.
